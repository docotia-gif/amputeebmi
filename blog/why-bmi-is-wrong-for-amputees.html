Why your BMI reads low after amputation

Why Your BMI Reads Low After Amputation — And Why It Matters

October 2026 · 5 min read

If you've ever checked your BMI after an amputation and felt a wave of relief at a "normal" reading, you should know: that number is almost certainly wrong. And the error isn't small — it can be the difference between "healthy weight" and "obese."

The Silent Underestimation

Standard BMI calculators use a formula that assumes an intact body. When you enter your current weight and height after limb loss, the calculator has no way of knowing that a significant fraction of your body mass is missing. It divides your reduced weight by your (unchanged) height squared and produces a number that looks fine — but isn't.

Consider a real example: A 5'10" man with a single above-knee amputation weighs 180 pounds. His standard BMI is 25.7 — technically "overweight" but not alarming. When corrected for the missing limb mass (an above-knee amputation removes about 16.3% of total body mass), his actual BMI is approximately 30.9 — solidly in the obese range.

That 5-point gap isn't a rounding error. It's the difference between being told to "watch your weight" and qualifying for bariatric surgery evaluation, medical nutrition therapy, or other weight-related interventions.

Why Most People Don't Know

The problem is invisible by design. Standard BMI calculators don't ask about amputation status. Electronic health records don't automatically apply corrections. Physicians who don't specialize in amputee care may not think to manually adjust. The result is a systematic, widespread underestimation of weight status in a population that's already at elevated risk for cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and metabolic syndrome.

Research has shown that people with limb loss have higher rates of obesity and related comorbidities than the general population. But if the studies use uncorrected BMI data, those rates may still be underestimates.

What You Can Do

First, use a corrected BMI calculator — like the one on this site — to get a more accurate baseline. The correction isn't perfect (it uses population averages), but it's dramatically more accurate than ignoring the issue.

Second, share your corrected BMI with your healthcare team. If you're working with a physician, dietitian, or rehabilitation specialist on weight management, give them the corrected number. It provides a more honest starting point for setting goals and tracking progress.

Third, if you're using BMI to track changes over time, always use the corrected version consistently. A 10-pound weight loss means something different when your baseline BMI was 25.7 vs. 30.9 — the corrected number gives you a truer picture of progress.

The Bottom Line

Standard BMI after amputation isn't just slightly off — it can be clinically misleading. Correcting for missing limb mass is a simple mathematical adjustment that produces a more accurate reflection of your weight status. It won't replace a comprehensive evaluation, but it's a far better starting point than the number a standard calculator gives you.
